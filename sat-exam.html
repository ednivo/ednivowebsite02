What is the SAT?

The SAT is a digital college-admissions test administered by College Board. It has two scored sections — Reading and Writing, and Math — and is used by many universities as one part of an undergraduate application. Admission policies vary by institution, so students should always check the testing policy of each university on their target list.

Digital & adaptive

The SAT is delivered through College Board's Bluebook testing application. Each section has two modules; performance on the first module determines the difficulty level of the second.

Two sections

Reading and Writing measures comprehension, vocabulary, analysis, revision and conventions. Math measures algebra, advanced math, data analysis, geometry and trigonometry.

No penalty for guessing

Students should answer every question. Unanswered questions do not earn points, so an educated guess is generally better than leaving an item blank.

Who uses SAT scores?

Universities decide independently whether and how to use SAT scores. Some are test-optional or test-free, while others may consider scores for admission, scholarships or placement.

SAT eligibility

College Board does not set a general minimum academic qualification or a universal age requirement for taking the SAT. In practice, the test is most commonly taken by students preparing for undergraduate applications. Students should check university requirements and their own application timeline before choosing a test date.

Planning tip: Students in Grades 10–12 can use their intended college application cycle to work backwards from the final SAT date they are comfortable with.

SAT exam pattern

The current digital SAT contains two sections. Each section is split into two equally timed modules, with a break between Reading and Writing and Math.

SectionTimeQuestions / tasks
Reading and Writing64 minutes54
Math70 minutes44
Total134 minutes98

Reading & Writing

Two 32-minute modules covering Information and Ideas, Craft and Structure, Expression of Ideas, and Standard English Conventions.

Math

Two 35-minute modules covering Algebra, Advanced Math, Problem-Solving and Data Analysis, and Geometry and Trigonometry. Some questions require students to enter their own answer.

SAT syllabus

The SAT content is organised into four Reading and Writing domains and four Math domains. Ednivo's complete checklist breaks these into study-ready skills.

Reading & Writing — Information and Ideas

Central ideas, details, evidence, inference and quantitative information presented with texts.

Reading & Writing — Craft and Structure

Words in context, text structure, purpose, rhetorical analysis and connections between texts.

Reading & Writing — Expression of Ideas

Revision, transitions, organisation, precision and rhetorical effectiveness.

Reading & Writing — Standard English Conventions

Sentence boundaries, grammar, usage and punctuation.

Math — Algebra

Linear equations, systems, inequalities and relationships represented algebraically.

Math — Advanced Math

Nonlinear equations, functions, equivalent expressions and advanced algebraic relationships.

Math — Problem-Solving & Data Analysis

Ratios, percentages, data, probability, statistics and interpreting quantitative claims.

Math — Geometry & Trigonometry

Area, volume, lines, angles, triangles, circles and right-triangle trigonometry.

How to prepare for the SAT

Effective SAT preparation is not simply about doing more questions. Start by understanding your current level, then allocate time to the skills that can produce the largest score improvement.

1. Establish a baseline

Use a full-length digital practice test to understand your starting score and section-level strengths.

2. Build concepts

Close content gaps in grammar, reading reasoning, algebra, advanced math and data analysis.

3. Practise deliberately

Work by skill and difficulty, review every mistake and keep an error log.

4. Simulate test day

Take timed full-length adaptive practice tests and build a pacing strategy before the real exam.

SAT cut-offs: what score should you target?

There is no single SAT cut-off that applies to every university. A competitive score depends on the institution, program, applicant pool and whether the university requires or considers scores.

Build a target range

Use the middle 50% or published score profile of your target universities where available, then set a personal target above your minimum comfort level.

Consider the full application

Grades, course rigor, essays, activities, recommendations and context can matter alongside testing. A score should be planned as one component of the application.

How to send SAT scores to universities

Students can send SAT scores to colleges and other organisations through their College Board account. Requirements vary, and some universities may have policies about self-reported or official scores, so always check each institution's admissions page.

Build your college list

Separate your list into ambitious, target and likely options, then check each institution's current testing policy.

Check deadlines

Make sure the SAT date and score-release timing fit the university's application deadline.

Send official scores when required

Follow the university's instructions and College Board's score-sending workflow where official reports are required.

Keep your application consistent

Use the same legal name and identifying information across your College Board account and application materials.
